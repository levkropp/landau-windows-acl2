## 00:00:00 — Why the signal's ceiling matters

Part Four built the smoothed signal G_z from prime powers, logarithm bounds, and weighted ramps. We can now compute rational intervals for its values. But why is this the signal that matters? The Landau bridge connects an upper ceiling on this signal to the location of zeta's zeros. This episode will unpack that sentence, starting with what a zero looks like on a map. Then we will explain the windows, and the reason a ceiling can work without a floor. We will check small pieces of the reasoning in ACL2 as we go. The full complex analytic theorem and these finite arithmetic examples will keep their own labels. This is Part Five of Landau Windows ACL Two: why a ceiling controls zeros.

## 00:00:48 — A complex number has two coordinates

A complex number gives us two real coordinates. The real part tells us how far to move horizontally. The imaginary part tells us how far to move vertically. We write the point as beta plus i times its height. The symbol i obeys the rule that i squared equals minus one. For the map in this episode, you can simply read the two coordinates as an address. A complex function takes an address and returns another complex number. A zero is an input address where the output has both coordinates equal to zero. It is not a point where just the real part of the output vanishes. The map will show the input addresses of zeros, rather than drawing a three-dimensional surface.

## 00:01:35 — The zeta function and the critical strip

The Riemann zeta function begins with a familiar-looking infinite sum. When the real part of its input is greater than one, add one divided by each positive integer raised to that input. This series does not define the function everywhere we need it. Complex analysis extends the function to the rest of the plane, apart from its pole at one. That extension is called analytic continuation. The nontrivial zeros lie in the strip between real part zero and real part one. The Riemann Hypothesis places all of those zeros on the vertical line at one half. The yellow dots here are sample known zeros from the original video's data. A finite collection of dots cannot establish a statement about every zero. The vertical line is the target, and the entire critical strip is the territory.

## 00:02:29 — Why excluding the right side is enough

The zero set has a reflection symmetry across the line at one half. If a nontrivial zero appeared to the left, a reflected zero would appear to the right at the same height. The purple dots in this illustration are hypothetical. We have not discovered an off-line zero. The symmetry comes from the functional equation together with complex conjugation. Therefore, excluding every zero to the right of one half also excludes every zero to its left. The only possible horizontal position left is one half. This is the mirror step from the original video. The Landau step will give us a way to exclude a region on the right, by controlling a real-valued signal instead.

## 00:03:15 — A ceiling controls only the upper side

Recall that G_z is the smoothed prime-power sum after subtracting its main term. It is a real number for each positive real input. A ceiling says that this number is less than or equal to some chosen upper bound. Here the bound is C times x to the power theta. The constant C is positive, and theta lies strictly between zero and one. The exponent determines the rate of growth allowed by the ceiling. At x equal to sixteen, x to the one-half power is four, while x to the three-quarter power is eight. The multiplier C can raise either ceiling. This animation borrows the original video's ceiling plot, and is a schematic illustration rather than computed values of G_z. A ceiling allows the signal to fall arbitrarily far below it. We are not assuming an absolute-value bound.

## 00:04:10 — A window is an entire interval

The ceiling does not have to hold at every sufficiently large input. The windowed theorem asks for long stretches where it holds. Start a stretch at Y, and end it at Y to the three-halves power. For Y equal to four, that gives the interval from four to eight. For Y equal to sixteen, the interval runs from sixteen to sixty-four. For Y equal to sixty-four, it runs all the way to five hundred and twelve. These are example addresses, not a claim that the actual signal obeys the ceiling on these particular intervals. Notice that the window grows in width as it moves out. Every real point in a selected window must satisfy the ceiling, including the points between any samples. A single successful sample is not a whole-window certificate.

## 00:05:01 — Read the quantifiers in their order

Now the important word: recurring. First fix the exponent theta and the positive constant C. Those two choices stay fixed across the windows. Someone can then demand any starting threshold, however large. Beyond that threshold, there must be some window on which the same ceiling holds throughout. The window does not have to begin exactly at the demanded threshold. There may be gaps between the good windows. But there cannot be a final good window after which they stop. The order of these choices matters. Choosing a new and larger C for each window would be a different hypothesis. We need arbitrarily large good windows for one fixed ceiling.

## 00:05:46 — The classical windowed Landau theorem

Here is the theorem with its conditions gathered together. Fix theta strictly between zero and one, and fix a positive constant C. Suppose the bound G_z of x is less than or equal to C times x to the power theta holds on arbitrarily large windows of the stated shape. Then zeta has no zero whose real part is greater than theta. In the picture, every zero must stay on or to the left of the boundary at theta. The repository proves this classical statement in Lean. This card shows the actual theorem statement from its source. The notation for a closed interval includes both endpoints, and the conclusion says that the zeta value is not zero. The proof supplies the analytic connection; the ceiling itself remains a hypothesis. Proving that hypothesis is a separate mathematical task.

## 00:06:42 — Turn the theorem around carefully

We can also read the theorem by contradiction. Suppose there were a zero with horizontal coordinate beta greater than theta. Fix a positive C. There could no longer be arbitrarily large windows on which the corresponding ceiling holds. In fact, beyond some threshold, every window of that shape would contain a point where the signal is strictly above the ceiling. This is the logical negation of having arbitrarily large good windows. It does not mean the signal is above the ceiling everywhere. It does not say that every sampled point detects a violation. And this classical existence statement has not yet computed the threshold or the violating address. Making that information explicit is why the arithmetic search and its error budgets matter.

## 00:07:32 — Use logarithmic addresses

To understand the proof's structure, it helps to change the horizontal coordinate. Write x as e to the power u, so u is the natural logarithm of x. Multiplication of x now becomes addition in the new coordinate. The window from Y to Y to the three-halves power becomes the interval from U to three-halves U, where U is the logarithm of Y. The power ceiling becomes C times e to the power theta u. Define the new signal g of u to mean G_z evaluated at e to the power u. No new prime-counting statistic has appeared; we have changed its address system. In logarithmic coordinates, the window has a width equal to one half of its starting address. That shape will fit the proof's concentrated weights.

## 00:08:20 — Growth, oscillation, and cancellation

The original video's waves give a useful first intuition. A hypothetical zero with real part beta suggests an oscillating contribution with a growth scale like e to the power beta u. Its height supplies an oscillation frequency. Move beta to the right, and the envelope grows faster. A ceiling with exponent theta grows more slowly when beta is greater than theta. This picture explains why a rightward zero should cause trouble. But the picture alone is not the proof. A cosine changes sign, and contributions from different zeros can cancel. Seeing one wave above a curve in a finite animation does not settle those issues. The rigorous argument uses a transform and estimates that can control the combined signal.

## 00:09:11 — A transform records weighted readings

A transform takes many weighted readings of the signal. Here we integrate g of u against e to the power minus s u, over nonnegative u. An integral is the continuous analogue of a weighted sum. The real part of s supplies exponential damping, while its imaginary part supplies an oscillating phase. Far enough to the right, in the region with real part greater than two, the crude growth bound makes this integral converge. Analytic continuation then connects the transform to a larger region. The crucial identity ties it to the logarithmic derivative of zeta. Near a zero of zeta, that identity produces a pole of the transform. Locally a pole looks like a nonzero constant divided by s minus the pole's address, plus a regular remainder. This is the precise bridge from a zero address to a feature of the transform.

## 00:10:07 — High derivatives focus the reading

Higher derivatives of the transform add powers of u to the weight. After normalization, the nonnegative kernel has the form u to the power k, times e to the power minus sigma u, divided by k factorial. The factorial is the product of the positive integers through k. For positive k, this kernel has its peak at u equal to k divided by sigma. Increasing k moves the region where the reading is most sensitive. The proof chooses the order in relation to the log-window, and estimates the weighted mass both inside and outside it. Our curves show normalized kernel shapes so their different heights fit on screen. Their absolute sizes are not being compared by this normalization. The actual estimates also include the signal's growth and the ceiling exponent. The outside tails are bounded, rather than silently thrown away.

## 00:11:04 — Why large negative values need no floor

The reason a one-sided estimate works starts with an elementary identity. Take a real number x and add it to its absolute value. If x is positive, the result is twice x. If x is negative, its absolute value cancels it and the result is zero. So the expression is twice the positive part of x, meaning the larger of x and zero. For minus three the result is zero… for two the result is four… and for minus one it is zero again. This is an identity about every real value. Our ACL2 teaching function represents the positive part on rational inputs. Its complete definition and the checked rational identity are shown here. An upper bound on x, when that bound is nonnegative, also bounds its positive part. No lower bound on x is needed for this step.

## 00:12:01 — Keep both weighted readings

Now use three signal values: minus three, two, and minus one. Give each weight one. The plain reading adds them, producing minus two. For a second reading, use phases one, minus one, and one. Multiplying by these phases produces minus three, minus two, and minus one, so the signed reading is minus six. Its absolute value is six. Add back the plain reading, and six plus minus two is four. The triangle inequality says that the absolute value of a sum is at most the sum of absolute values. Combining that fact with the positive-part identity bounds this combined reading by twice the sum of the positive contributions. Here the only positive signal value was two, and twice two is four. If the first signal value falls from minus three to minus ten, the combined reading is still four. The negative value disappeared from this upper bound; we did not need to put a floor under it.

## 00:13:04 — The finite cancellation in ACL2

Here are the two ACL2 functions used for this finite example. The plain function adds its three rational inputs. The signed function subtracts the middle input instead of adding it. These fixed real phases are a small special case, chosen so we can see every calculation. The general teaching theorem allows all three rational signal values to vary. It bounds the absolute signed reading plus the plain reading by twice the sum of their positive parts. There is no nonnegativity hypothesis on the signal values. The project also has a disk-based version for complex phases and nonnegative weights. For the continuous transform, the same idea controls the magnitude of an oscillating reading plus a plain real reading. That extra real reading must be retained. Analytic estimates must control it before one can turn the combined estimate into a bound on the oscillating reading alone.

## 00:14:04 — A nearby pole forces large coefficients

The other side of the argument comes from the pole. Expand the transform around an ordinary point to the right of the forbidden region. Its successive coefficients record its higher derivatives, with factorial normalization. For a simple pole at distance d, the pole contribution has coefficient magnitudes proportional to one divided by successive powers of d. A more distant regular remainder contributes a slower geometric scale once an enclosing circle has been justified. In a favorable isolated picture, the zero is closer to our expansion point than the boundary at theta. That smaller distance makes the pole's coefficient scale grow faster relative to the ceiling scale. This diagram is a local schematic, not a certificate for an actual zero. A complete proof must arrange the geometry and control every competing pole and the regular remainder. Multiple zeros and clusters cannot be ignored. Those obligations will return when we build the pole and circle certificates.

## 00:15:10 — Incompatible geometric scales

After the analytic estimates are in place, the final contradiction has an arithmetic shape. The same coefficient cannot be at least a lower estimate and at most a smaller upper estimate. If one geometric scale grows by a factor greater than one relative to the other, repeated multiplication eventually exceeds any fixed target. For example, multiply repeatedly by three halves. The values at exponents zero, two, four, and six are one… nine quarters… eighty-one sixteenths… and seven hundred twenty-nine sixty-fourths. By the sixth exponent this example has passed eight. This demonstrates the geometric-growth mechanism. It does not derive the analytic estimates that feed it. We still need justified coefficients, remainders, and constants before applying this mechanism to the transform.

## 00:16:06 — Compute a sufficient order

An existence argument says that some sufficiently large order will work. The arithmetic version asks us to compute a sufficient order. The repository's geometric index does this for a rational growth factor greater than one and a nonnegative rational target. Its definition uses the precision fuel introduced in Part Four. For growth factor three halves and target eight, it returns seventeen. That is a conservative bound, not the first crossing we saw in the example. The proof uses a linear lower estimate for repeated multiplication: the kth power is at least one plus k times the quantity growth factor minus one. At order seventeen, that lower estimate is nineteen halves, already greater than eight. The checked theorem guarantees that every natural order at least seventeen reaches the target. This is a real computed component of the project. It still needs the analytic data and inequalities supplied to the larger argument.

## 00:17:10 — Why ceilings near one half would suffice

Return to the line at one half. Suppose the required recurring-window ceilings were available for every exponent just above one half. For each such exponent, its positive constant may be different, but it must stay fixed across that exponent's windows. A hypothetical zero with real part beta greater than one half leaves a positive gap. Choose epsilon to be half that gap. The exponent one half plus epsilon then lies strictly between one half and beta. The Landau theorem at that exponent excludes the supposed zero. For a concrete illustration, take beta equal to three quarters. Epsilon is one eighth, and the chosen exponent is five eighths. The ACL2 card checks this midpoint inequality for any rational beta in the stated range. That arithmetic fact chooses the exponent in our example; the zero-free conclusion still uses the analytic theorem. Excluding every zero on the right, together with reflection, puts every nontrivial zero on the central line. This explains what the ceiling-building part of an RH proof has to deliver; this episode has not constructed those ceilings.

## 00:18:24 — What is being brought into arithmetic

We can now name the job of this repository more clearly. The classical Landau theorem turns recurring ceilings into a zero-free region. The arithmetic project aims to replace the relevant existence steps with explicit constructions and checked bounds. The finite search, rational signal evaluator, one-sided estimates, and computed orders each address part of that job. A proof of an isolated arithmetic lemma is not yet the full reduction. ## 00:19:24 — From the bridge to explicit kernels

The central idea is now in view. A long window limits the positive part of the signal where a carefully chosen reading concentrates. The one-sided identity keeps large negative signal values from spoiling that combined upper bound. A nearby pole supplies a competing coefficient scale. Once all the geometric and tail estimates are justified, those scales become incompatible at a sufficiently large order. The arithmetic challenge is to make that order, the bounds, and the resulting witness construction explicit. Next we will build the exponentially damped kernels and their finite approximations.
