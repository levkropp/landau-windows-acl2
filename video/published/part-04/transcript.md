## 00:00:58 — Primes and positive powers of primes

A prime is a whole number at least two whose only positive divisors are one and itself. A prime power is a prime raised to a positive whole-number exponent. Two, four, eight, and sixteen are powers of the same prime, two. Thirteen also counts: it is thirteen to the first power. Six does not count, because its prime factors include both two and three. One does not count either; the exponent must be positive. Sixteen gives a useful warning: it is also four squared, but four is not a prime base. The prime-power description we want is two to the fourth power. The weight we will attach is called the von Mangoldt function, written with a capital Lambda. At a prime power, its value is the natural logarithm of the prime base. At other positive integers, its value is zero. So every positive power of two receives the weight log two… not the logarithm of the whole power. We will explain the logarithm calculation shortly; first we need a bounded way to recognize the prime powers.

## 00:05:33 — A natural logarithm from finite rectangles

The logarithm in this construction is the natural logarithm. For an input at least one, it is the area under the curve one over t, from one to the input. Use input two as a small example. Split the interval from one to two into four equal pieces, each of width one quarter. The curve decreases, so a rectangle using the right endpoint lies below the area on its piece. A rectangle using the left endpoint lies above it. Add the four smaller rectangles to obtain a lower bound… and the four larger rectangles to obtain an upper bound. Those rectangle areas are rational numbers. The lower sum is five hundred and thirty-three over eight hundred and forty. The upper sum is three hundred and nineteen over four hundred and twenty. The true log of two lies between them. Finer partitions give a way to control the gap between the bounds. The area interpretation is a mathematical theorem; the arithmetic calculation consists of the finite rational sums we are about to define.

## 00:10:00 — Each weight turns on through a ramp

Now fix a positive integer n and let x move along the horizontal axis. The coefficient for that index is the larger of zero and x divided by n minus one. Before x reaches n, the coefficient is zero. At x equal to n, it is still zero. After that point, it grows linearly. Here is the complete one-line definition of lw-ramp. For n equal to four, move x from three and a half… to four… and then to four and a half. The corresponding coefficients are zero… zero… and one eighth. The maximum operation is what keeps the contribution turned off before its starting point. We multiply this ramp by the von Mangoldt weight at n. The prime-power pattern supplies the weight, and the ramp supplies the dependence on x.

## 00:10:57 — Assemble the smoothed prime-power sum

## 00:14:08 — The real sum is continuous, with corners

## 00:15:04 — Introduce the constant in the centering term

To center the sum, we need one more constant: Euler's constant, written gamma. It is defined by the limiting difference between a harmonic sum and the natural logarithm. The harmonic sum at a positive whole number is one, plus one half, plus one third, and so on up to its reciprocal. Here is lw-harmonic, the exact rational recursion for that finite sum. Build its first values: one… three halves… eleven sixths… and twenty-five twelfths. Subtracting log of the count gives a sequence approaching gamma, whose familiar decimal begins zero point five seven seven. We use a finite inequality to turn that limiting definition into usable bounds. For a positive count, gamma lies between the harmonic sum minus log of the count minus its reciprocal… and the harmonic sum minus log of the count. The gap in this ideal interval is one over the count. The logarithm itself has a rational enclosure, so we must propagate that uncertainty too. We are enclosing a real constant with rational endpoints, rather than asserting that the constant is one of those endpoints.
